Automated Gantry-Based Disassembly System

Context
Bachelor thesis, 2026
Team
2 engineers
Supervisor
Tobias Eifler
My focus
Mechanics, sensing & control
Read the full thesis · PDF · 100 pp

Remanufacturing recovers value from worn-out products by taking them apart so their parts can be reused, but that disassembly is still done almost entirely by hand, because every product that comes in is a little different. My bachelor thesis, done together with DTU's national REMANUFACTORY research centre, asked a simple question: could one machine handle the repetitive part of that job and still hand the awkward cases back to a person?

Our answer was a unified tool head. It's a single 3D-printed module, driven by one shaft, that both unscrews fasteners and presses parts out without ever swapping tools. That's trickier than it sounds, since the same assembly has to deliver twisting force for the screws and pushing force for the press at the same time. We managed it with an internal spring that lets the head slide passively from screw mode into press mode as it presses down, with no motor and no reconfiguration. It even swaps its own screwdriver bits at a little parking station, using nothing but the axes the machine already has.

Getting it to work reliably turned out to be mostly a sensing and software problem. A camera finds every screw, corrects for lens distortion and viewing angle with a homography calibration, and reports each screw's position in millimetres along with its size (M4 vs M5). A laser distance sensor watches the head come down and can tell normal surface contact apart from the tiny extra bit of travel that means the bit has actually seated in the screw. A load cell keeps an eye on the press force and stops the instant it hits a set limit. Tying it all together is a state machine that checks the sensors at every step and either carries on, tries again, or flags the part for a human. The machine never just pushes blindly.

We deliberately built the prototype in 3D-printed PLA so a failed part could be redesigned and reprinted the same day, but dimensioned every component so the exact same geometry can be re-cut in 10 mm metal plate for a stronger production version, without moving a single sensor. REGBOT Self-Balancing Robot

Context
Course project, 2026
Team
4 engineers
Domain
Classical control
Tools
MATLAB · Simulink
Read the full report · PDF

A REGBOT is a two-wheeled robot that has to actively balance to stay upright, a bit like a Segway the size of a lunchbox. Left on its own it just falls over; the only thing holding it up is a controller nudging the wheels hundreds of times a second. For this control-design course we designed and tuned the whole control system that keeps it balanced while it drives and moves to target positions.

The system is built as four nested control loops, each one wrapped around the one inside it: a fast inner loop for wheel speed, a balance loop that holds the tilt angle, then a velocity loop, and finally a position loop on the outside. We designed each loop in the frequency domain, picking a target phase margin, reading the response straight off the Bode plots and sizing the controller from there. Then we modelled and linearised the whole robot in MATLAB and Simulink so we could check the design before it ever touched hardware.

Balancing is unstable by nature, so the tilt dynamics needed a post-integrator just to become controllable, with a PI-Lead controller on top to hold the angle without overshooting. The robot also has an odd habit called non-minimum-phase behaviour, where to drive forward it first has to tip backward a little. We handled that with a pre-filter that eases each command in gently.

The part I learned the most from was the gap between theory and reality. The clean simulated design misbehaved on the real robot, so we hand-tuned every loop on the hardware and found out exactly which effects the tidy linear model had quietly left out, like friction, small time delays and sensor noise. In the end it balanced its way through a full closed square-path mission. Watch the full mission run →

  • Cascaded controller stack. PI wheel-speed loop, a post-integrator to stabilise the inherently unstable tilt, a PI-Lead balance controller, and P position control.
  • Undershoot handling. Added a first-order pre-filter to tame the robot's non-minimum-phase response so the balance loop had time to react.
  • Simulation vs. hardware. Directly compared modelled and measured step responses, then reconciled them through tuning.
  • Full mission. The finished robot balanced while completing a closed square-path driving mission.
Technical skills
  • Classical control design. Frequency-response methods, phase margin, lead/lag compensation and building loops inside loops.
  • Modelling and simulation. Linearising an unstable robot in MATLAB and Simulink.
  • Getting theory onto hardware. Working out why a real robot behaves differently from its model, and closing that gap step by step.
Ways of working
  • Patient, systematic tuning: changing one parameter at a time and watching what the robot does.
  • Splitting a four-person control problem into loops the team could design in parallel.

AI Robotic Arm for Waste Sorting

Context
Project work, 2025
Team
5 engineers
Supervisor
Konstantinos Poulios
Tools
SolidWorks · IKPy · OpenAI
Read the full report · PDF · in Danish

This is a 5-axis robotic arm that sorts waste on its own, aimed at the kind of manual, and often unsafe, sorting that happens in low-resource areas. A camera looks down at the work area, an AI model works out what each object is, and the arm picks it up and drops it in the right bin, for example keeping electronic waste apart from everything else.

Before building anything, we took the time to choose the concept properly. A morphological analysis compared different grippers and ways of driving the joints, and a torque calculation (a peak of roughly 6.7 N·m at the shoulder) made it clear that cheap stepper motors just couldn't lift the target load. So the arm ended up servo-driven, with each joint's motor sized to what that joint actually has to hold. The whole thing is 3D-printed, designed around the tolerances the printer can really hold.

To get the tip to a chosen point in space, we used inverse kinematics, the maths that works backwards from a target position to every joint angle. We fed the IKPy library a URDF robot model exported from our SolidWorks CAD, and a Raspberry Pi camera (calibrated against ArUco markers) turns what it sees into real-world X-Y coordinates for the arm to aim at. An AI image-recognition model then labels the object so the arm knows which bin it belongs in.

Two problems taught us the most, and both lived on the messy line between the digital model and the physical robot. First, the servos didn't actually go where they were told, since each one had its own offset and scaling. We fixed that with a measured per-motor calibration function that turns the computed angle into the right command. Second, the servos' 180° limits kept trapping the solver in dead-ends, so we took those limits out of the model and handled them in our own code by giving the solver sensible starting guesses.
